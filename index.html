Student experience in Australian higher education

What makes a different university experience?

Student experience isn't one number. It varies with what you study, how you attend, and who studies alongside you.

1

Is the experience changing?

Just under four in five undergraduates rated their overall experience positively before the pandemic. In 2020, when teaching moved online almost overnight, that fell by ten percentage points in a single year, from 78.5% to 68.7%. Every gap on this page is measured the same way: one share of students subtracted from another.

The recovery has been uneven. Postgraduate coursework ratings climbed back and kept going. Undergraduate ratings stalled around 76 and have not moved for three years. The two groups have swapped places.

Both levels lost about ten points in 2020. Only postgraduate coursework has since climbed past where it started.

2

What does “experience” actually mean?

The survey doesn't ask one question. It asks about five separate things, and students answer them very differently.

  • Learning resourcesLibraries, online systems, labs, study spaces.
  • Skills developmentWhether the course builds skills students can use.
  • Teaching quality and engagementWhether teaching is clear, organised and engaging.
  • Student support and servicesWhether help is there when it's needed.
  • Peer engagementHow often students work and talk with each other.

Learning resources lead at 83%, peer engagement trails at 61% — a 22-point spread inside the same survey.

Does what you study matter?

20 points separate the highest and lowest study areas — agriculture and environmental studies at 86.6%, computing and information systems at 66.7%

Those differences aren't the same at both levels. Six study areas move far enough to change where they sit among the 21. Three fall and three climb, and they are coloured individually below.

Medicine is the clearest. It ranks fifth of 21 among undergraduates and twentieth among postgraduate coursework students — a fall of eleven points. Veterinary science and rehabilitation drop about nine points each.

The climbs are smaller but they rearrange the top of the chart. Humanities and culture goes from eleventh to first, nursing from fifteenth to third, business and management from eighteenth to seventh, each gaining about six points.

The fifteen grey lines are every other field, and most of them barely move: eight shift by less than three points. Whatever separates medicine's two levels is not a general rule about studying at postgraduate level.

Study areas don't simply rate well or badly overall — each has a shape. Medicine undergraduates report the highest peer engagement in the country at 83.1% while rating support below average. Science and mathematics does the reverse.

The six fields coloured in the chart above keep their colours here, so you can follow the same field through both. They do not have matching shapes: medicine is strong on peer engagement and weak on support, while humanities and culture is the other way round. Moving up or down between levels says nothing about which parts of the experience a field does well.

What holds everywhere is the dip on the right. Every one of the 21 fields rates peer engagement lowest or close to it, whichever level you switch to, and the national line drops with them.

Almost every line drops at the right-hand end. Whatever a field does well, peer engagement is where it does worst.

Could geography explain it?

If the experience varies this much by field of study, perhaps it varies by place too. Students are certainly not spread evenly.

New South Wales and Victoria together hold roughly three-fifths of Australia's 1.64 million students, and that concentration sharpens further inside the cities.

New South Wales enrols 545,124 students and Victoria 470,742. Queensland is next at 249,000, then Western Australia at 162,000. Below that the numbers fall away quickly: Tasmania enrols 32,000 and the Northern Territory 13,000, each with a single university.

But the experience itself barely moves. On overall educational experience, undergraduate ratings run from 73.6% in the Australian Capital Territory to 78.9% in Tasmania.

That is five points across the country, against twenty across study areas. Five of the six measures behave this way, and the map stays nearly colourless in each.

Peer engagement is the exception. Choose it and two jurisdictions fall out of the pack: the Northern Territory at 35.8% and Tasmania at 43.6%, against 61.4% nationally. Both rest on one university each, so both swing between levels.

Geography barely shifts five of the six measures. The sixth separates the states by 28 points.

Geography tells you where students are. With one exception it tells you little about how their experience differs — and that exception is about who is in the room, not where the room is.

Who is in the room, and how do they attend?

If place doesn't explain the differences, the people might. Undergraduate and postgraduate coursework cohorts aren't the same students at different stages — they are substantially different populations.

The two levels are not one pipeline. Domestic respondents fall from 170,244 at undergraduate to 52,319 at postgraduate coursework, a drop of roughly seven in ten.

International respondents travel the opposite way, climbing from 46,383 to 70,625. Postgraduate coursework therefore holds more international students than undergraduate does, from a cohort little more than half the size.

Postgraduate coursework is the one cohort on this page where domestic students are in the minority.

That cohort comes overwhelmingly from a handful of countries. Five of them account for 61.7% of all overseas students in Australian higher education.

China is the largest single source at 166,525. But South Asia outweighs it as a region: India, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Pakistan and Bhutan together send 195,444.

Composition lines up loosely with section 3. The study areas where postgraduates gain most on undergraduates — computing, business, engineering — are also the most heavily international. The link is far from exact: nursing has one of the smallest international cohorts and one of the largest gains.

Nearly every route on this map begins in Asia. Outside it, no country sends more than 8,200 students.

How students attend matters too. Students who attended every class on campus rated teaching quality 82.3% positively; those entirely online, 75.1%.

On-campus attendance leads on all six measures, at both levels. But the margin is usually small, and on one measure it is not small at all: peer engagement separates the two by tens of points.

This is an association rather than a cause. Online and on-campus cohorts differ in subject mix, in age, and in whatever else occupies their time.

Fully online postgraduates rate peer engagement at 35.4%, against 79.6% on campus. That 44-point gap is the widest anywhere on this page.

Which differences are actually biggest?

Put the three lenses on one axis. The thick bar covers the middle 80% of each set of groups; the thin line covers the full range.

Through the middle 80%, study areas span 11 points, universities 9, and states and territories 4. The thin line behind each bar reaches further still, out to the single most extreme group.

Against the full 0–100 scale all three look narrow. None of this establishes cause either: these are students' own ratings, compared across groups that differ in many other ways at once.

The lens you choose changes the size of the difference you find. Field of study is almost three times as wide as geography.

It isn't just where you study. The experience varies with what you study, how you attend, and who studies alongside you.
